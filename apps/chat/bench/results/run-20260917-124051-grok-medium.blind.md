## reason-logic
*Mislabeled Apples/Oranges/Mixed boxes; one fruit pick to fix all labels?*

### A

Pick one fruit from the box labeled Mixed (the only guaranteed pure box). Whatever fruit you draw is that box’s true contents. The remaining two labels then swap onto the remaining two boxes by elimination (each is still wrong, so the box labeled with the fruit you did *not* draw must be Mixed, and the last box is the leftover pure fruit).

This works on every draw; drawing from either of the other boxes can leave an ambiguity.

### B

**Pick one fruit from the box labeled “Mixed.”**

All three labels are wrong, so the box labeled Mixed cannot be mixed—it must be pure Apples or pure Oranges. Drawing one fruit tells you which:

- Suppose you draw an apple. Then that box is Apples.
- The box labeled Apples cannot be apples, and it also cannot be mixed (the only remaining pure option that keeps every label wrong is Oranges). So it is Oranges.
- The last box (labeled Oranges) must therefore be Mixed.

(The symmetric case holds if you draw an orange instead.)  

One draw from the Mixed-labeled box is enough to deduce the true contents of all three and relabel them correctly.

### C

**Pick one fruit from the box labeled "Mixed".**

### Why this works (and why the other boxes don't)

All three labels are **wrong**, so:
- The "Apples" box contains **either** all oranges **or** mixed.
- The "Oranges" box contains **either** all apples **or** mixed.
- The "Mixed" box contains **either** all apples **or** all oranges (it cannot be mixed).

The "Mixed" box is the only one that gives you definitive information from a single draw.

#### Case 1: You draw an **apple** from the "Mixed" box
- That box **must** be all apples (it can't be mixed, and you just pulled an apple).
- So we now know: **"Mixed" label → Apples**.

Remaining boxes: labeled "Apples" and "Oranges".  
Remaining contents: all oranges and mixed.

- The box labeled "Oranges" **cannot** be oranges (all labels are wrong).  
  Therefore it must be the **mixed** box.
- The last box (labeled "Apples") must therefore be **oranges**.

#### Case 2: You draw an **orange** from the "Mixed" box
- That box **must** be all oranges.
- So we now know: **"Mixed" label → Oranges**.

Remaining boxes: labeled "Apples" and "Oranges".  
Remaining contents: all apples and mixed.

- The box labeled "Apples" **cannot** be apples.  
  Therefore it must be the **mixed** box.
- The last box (labeled "Oranges") must therefore be **apples**.

In either case, one draw from the "Mixed" box lets you correctly relabel all three boxes with certainty.

### Why you shouldn't pick from the other boxes
If you pick from the "Apples" or "Oranges" box instead, a single fruit doesn't tell you whether that box is pure or mixed. You end up with ambiguity and cannot deduce the other two labels with 100% confidence. The "Mixed" box is the only one that breaks the ambiguity immediately.

### D

**Pick from the box labeled "Mixed".**

Here's why this works (and why it must be this box):

All three boxes are mislabeled, so:
- The "Apples" box is **not** apples.
- The "Oranges" box is **not** oranges.
- The "Mixed" box is **not** mixed (it must therefore be either pure apples or pure oranges).

**Step-by-step deduction after drawing one fruit from the "Mixed" box:**

1. Suppose you draw an **apple**.  
   - The "Mixed" box cannot be mixed, so it must be **pure Apples**.  
   - The box labeled "Oranges" cannot be oranges (wrong label). It also cannot be apples (that's already identified). Therefore it must be **Mixed**.  
   - This leaves the box labeled "Apples", which must be **pure Oranges**.

2. Suppose you draw an **orange**.  
   - The "Mixed" box must be **pure Oranges**.  
   - The box labeled "Apples" cannot be apples. It also cannot be oranges (that's already identified). Therefore it must be **Mixed**.  
   - This leaves the box labeled "Oranges", which must be **pure Apples**.

In both cases, one draw tells you the contents of all three boxes with certainty.

**Why you can't reliably do it from either of the other boxes:**  
If you pick from the "Apples" or "Oranges" box instead, you can end up with a draw that leaves two equally possible configurations, and you won't know which is correct. Only the "Mixed" box guarantees that whatever you draw eliminates all ambiguity.

### E

Pick one fruit from the box labeled "Mixed".

Since all labels are wrong, this box cannot contain a mix—it must contain either only apples or only oranges. Suppose the fruit you draw is an apple. Then the box must be all apples (and you can relabel it accordingly).

## reason-math
*Two trains SP↔Rio (430 km), 90 km/h @8:00 vs 110 km/h @8:30 — when meet?*

### A

**They meet at 10:25:30 (10:25:30 a.m.).**

Let \( t \) be the time in hours after 8:00 when the trains meet.

- Train A travels for \( t \) hours at 90 km/h, covering \( 90t \) km.
- Train B starts at 8:30, so it travels for \( (t - 0.5) \) hours at 110 km/h, covering \( 110(t - 0.5) \) km.

The sum of distances equals 430 km:

\[
90t + 110(t - 0.5) = 430
\]

Expand and simplify:

\[
90t + 110t - 55 = 430
\]
\[
200t = 485
\]
\[
t = \frac{485}{200} = 2.425 \text{ hours}
\]

Convert 2.425 hours: 2 hours + \( 0.425 \times 60 = 25.5 \) minutes.

Thus, 8:00 + 2 h 25.5 min = **10:25:30**.

(Alternative check: After the 30-min head start, Train A covers 45 km, leaving 385 km. Combined speed is 200 km/h, so \( 385 / 200 = 1.925 \) h after 8:30 also yields 10:25:30.)

### B

The first train travels \(90\times0.5=45\) km in the half-hour before the second train starts, leaving \(430-45=385\) km still to be covered.

From 8:30 onward the trains close the remaining distance at a combined speed of \(90+110=200\) km/h, which takes
\[
\frac{385}{200}=1.925\text{ h}=1\text{ h }55.5\text{ min}.
\]
Adding that interval to 8:30 yields 10:25:30.

(Equivalently, if \(t\) is hours after 8:00 the meeting condition is \(90t+110(t-0.5)=430\), so \(t=2.425\) h after 8:00, again 10:25:30.)

Final answer: 10:25:30

## reason-compare
*SQLite vs PostgreSQL for self-hosted single-user app, <150 words.*

### A

SQLite is usually best for a self-hosted single-user web app. It’s embedded (no daemon), one file, tiny RAM/CPU, and backups are a file copy. WAL handles modest concurrent access. Ideal for simple apps with light writes.

Choose PostgreSQL for concurrent writers (jobs/workers), JSONB/FTS/extensions, complex queries, or possible later multi-user growth. Tradeoff: extra process, more memory, more ops.

Default to SQLite; migrate only if you hit its limits.

### B

**SQLite is usually the better choice.**

For a self-hosted single-user web app, **SQLite** wins on simplicity, zero configuration, minimal resource use, and easy backups (just copy one file). With WAL mode, it easily handles typical web workloads from a single user with excellent performance and reliability.

**PostgreSQL** is only worth the overhead if you need its advanced features: powerful JSONB, sophisticated full-text search, complex constraints, window functions, or extensions (PostGIS, etc.). It requires running and maintaining a separate database server, using more memory and adding deployment complexity.

**Rule of thumb:**
- Default to **SQLite** (90% of cases).
- Choose **PostgreSQL** only when you specifically need its advanced capabilities or anticipate growing into multi-user usage.

### C

**SQLite** is usually the right default for a self-hosted single-user web app.

- Zero config, single file, no server process
- Excellent read performance, solid writes for one user
- Tiny resource use, easy backups (copy the file)
- Great with WAL mode + a connection pooler if needed

**Choose PostgreSQL** when you need:
- Complex queries, heavy concurrency (even if “single user” has background jobs), or strict multi-process write patterns
- Advanced types, full-text, JSONB indexing, extensions, or future multi-user growth
- Stronger operational tooling and replication

**Rule of thumb:** Start with SQLite unless you already know you’ll outgrow it. Many single-user apps never do. Switch to Postgres only for clear feature or concurrency needs—not “just in case.”

### D

SQLite is usually the better choice for a self-hosted single-user web app.

**SQLite** is serverless, uses a single file, and requires almost no setup or resources. It excels at simplicity, fast local reads/writes, and trivial backups. For one user, its concurrency limitations are rarely relevant.

**PostgreSQL** is overkill in most cases. It needs a running server, more memory, and ongoing maintenance. Use it only when you need specific features like advanced full-text search, complex JSON operations, certain extensions, or if you expect to add more users later.

**Rule of thumb**: Start with SQLite. Switch to PostgreSQL only if you hit a concrete limitation.

### E

**SQLite** is usually the right call for a self-hosted single-user web app. It’s zero-config, file-based, has negligible overhead, and delivers excellent performance for low-concurrency workloads. Backups are trivial (copy the file), and you avoid running any database server.

**PostgreSQL** becomes preferable when you need advanced features (complex queries, JSONB, full-text search, extensions), stronger concurrency guarantees, or a clear path to multi-user use later. It adds setup and resource cost that is usually unnecessary for one user.

Choose SQLite for simplicity and speed of development; switch to PostgreSQL only if specific capabilities or future growth justify the extra management.

## write-summary
*Explain prompt caching in two short paragraphs, non-technical.*

### A
