(The symmetric argument holds if you drew an orange.)

Any other choice leaves an ambiguity that a single fruit cannot resolve.

### B

**Pick one fruit from the box labeled "Mixed."**

All three boxes are mislabeled, so the true contents are one all-apples, one all-oranges, and one mixed, but no label is correct:
- “Apples” is not apples (it is oranges or mixed),
- “Oranges” is not oranges (it is apples or mixed),
- “Mixed” is not mixed (it is pure apples or pure oranges).

Because the “Mixed” label is wrong, that box contains only one kind of fruit. Drawing a single fruit from it immediately tells you which pure fruit it actually holds.

- Suppose you draw an apple. Then the box labeled “Mixed” is really **Apples**.
- The box labeled “Oranges” cannot be oranges and cannot be apples (already identified), so it must be **Mixed**.
- The remaining box (labeled “Apples”) must therefore be **Oranges**.

(The symmetric case holds if you draw an orange instead: “Mixed” is really Oranges, “Apples” must be Mixed, and “Oranges” is Apples.)

One draw from the mislabeled “Mixed” box thus lets you correctly relabel all three. Drawing from either of the other boxes leaves an ambiguity that a single fruit cannot resolve.

*Two trains SP↔Rio (430 km), 90 km/h @8:00 vs 110 km/h @8:30 — when meet?*

### A

**They meet at 10:25:30.**

Let \( t \) be the time in hours after 8:00 when the trains meet.

- Distance covered by first train: \( 90t \) km  
- Second train starts 0.5 hours later, so it travels for \( (t - 0.5) \) hours: \( 110(t - 0.5) \) km  

Equation:  
\[ 90t + 110(t - 0.5) = 430 \]  

Expand:  
\[ 90t + 110t - 55 = 430 \]  
\[ 200t = 485 \]  
\[ t = 2.425 \] hours  

2.425 hours after 8:00 = 2 hours + 0.425 × 60 minutes = **10:25:30**.

### B

The first train has a 30-minute head start and covers \(90\times0.5=45\) km in that time, leaving \(430-45=385\) km. After 8:30 the trains close the remaining gap at a combined speed of \(90+110=200\) km/h, which takes
\[
\frac{385}{200}=1.925\text{ h}=1\text{ h }55.5\text{ min}.
\]
Adding that interval to 8:30 yields 10:25:30.

Equivalently, let \(t\) be hours after 8:00. The meeting condition is
\[
90t+110(t-0.5)=430\implies200t=485\implies t=2.425\text{ h}=2\text{ h }25.5\text{ min},
\]
which is again 10:25:30.

Final answer: 10:25:30

Let \( t \) be the time in hours after 8:00 when the trains meet.

## reason-compare
*SQLite vs PostgreSQL for self-hosted single-user app, <150 words.*

### A

**SQLite** is usually the right default: zero-config file DB, tiny footprint, trivial backups (copy the file), and more than enough for one user even with WAL concurrency. Pick it for simplicity, portability, low resources, and datasets that stay modest.

**PostgreSQL** wins if you need concurrent writers (background jobs + many tabs), richer SQL/JSON/extensions, stricter isolation, or you already run it. Extra process, RAM, and ops cost are the tradeoff.

Start with SQLite; migrate later if you actually hit its limits.

### B

**SQLite vs PostgreSQL for Single-User Self-Hosted Apps**

**SQLite** is usually the correct choice. It's zero-config, embedded, and trivial to deploy—just a single file. Backups are as simple as copying the file. It has negligible resource usage and perfectly handles single-user concurrency. Modern SQLite with WAL mode is fast enough for nearly all personal web apps.

**PostgreSQL** makes sense only when you specifically need its advanced features: superior JSONB handling, powerful full-text search, advanced indexing, or extensions (PostGIS, Timescale, etc.). It adds significant operational overhead (managing a server, memory tuning, backups).

**Rule of thumb:**
- Default to **SQLite** (90% of cases).
- Choose **PostgreSQL** only if you have a clear feature requirement that SQLite can't satisfy well.

## write-summary
*Explain prompt caching in two short paragraphs, non-technical.*

### A

Imagine chatting with an AI that has to re-read your entire conversation from the very first message every single time you send something new. Prompt caching is like giving the AI a bookmark: it saves its understanding of the earlier parts so it only has to process what’s actually new.

That shortcut makes replies faster and cheaper, especially in long chats or when the same instructions are reused, without changing how the AI thinks or what it says.

### B

Prompt caching is a technique that lets AI language models remember and reuse parts of the instructions you give them. Instead of reading the same long prompt from scratch every time, the model can quickly pull up the already-processed version, much like how a person might remember the setup of a recurring conversation rather than starting over.

This makes responses faster and cheaper, especially when you're asking multiple questions that share the same background information or rules. The model only has to do the heavy work once for the repeated part, then focuses its effort on your new question.

### C

**Prompt caching** is a way for AI chat systems to work smarter and faster with repeated or long messages. When you send the same instructions, background info, or conversation history over and over, the system can temporarily “remember” (cache) the parts that stay the same instead of re-reading and re-thinking them from scratch every single time.  

This saves computing effort, which usually means quicker replies and lower costs—especially useful in ongoing chats, apps that reuse the same setup, or whenever large chunks of text don’t change between requests. It’s like a cook prepping common ingredients once and reusing them for several meals rather than starting over each time.

### D

Prompt caching is a way for AI models like ChatGPT to work faster and use less computing power. Instead of reading and processing the entire prompt from scratch every time, the model can remember the work it already did on parts that stay the same.
